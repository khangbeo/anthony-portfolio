Support work is a strange kind of tiring. It is not physical, but a day of back to back calls, each with someone who is frustrated about something different, leaves your head buzzing. Early on, I would finish a shift feeling drained in a way I could not explain, and I would carry the stress of one call straight into the next one. Mindfulness sounds like a buzzword, but the simple version of it fixed a lot of that for me.

## Resetting between calls

The habit that helped most is also the smallest. Between calls, I take about thirty seconds to actually finish the last one in my head. I write the note, I take one slow breath, and I remind myself the next person knows nothing about the previous call and does not deserve its mood. It sounds minor, but that tiny gap stopped hard calls from bleeding into easy ones, and my easy calls got better because I was actually present for them.

## Noticing instead of reacting

Mindfulness, the way I practice it, is mostly about noticing. Noticing my shoulders are up around my ears. Noticing I am rereading the same log line for the fourth time because I am tired, not because it is complicated. Once I notice it, I can do something about it: stand up, get water, look at something far away for a minute. Before, I would just push through and make sloppy mistakes, then wonder why.

## Protecting the off hours

The other half is boundaries. When work follows you home in your head, you never really recover, and the next day starts at half charge. I am not perfect at it, but I try to close the day deliberately: notes written, loose ends listed for tomorrow, then done. Rest is not a reward for finishing everything. It is part of how the work gets done well.

None of this requires an app or a retreat. It is just paying attention on purpose, in small moments, during a job that constantly pulls your attention in ten directions. For work like ours, that might be the most practical skill there is.
