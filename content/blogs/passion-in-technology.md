I did not grow up planning a career in technology. For a long time, art was my thing. I drew constantly, and if you had asked me as a teenager what I would be doing as an adult, I would have said something with a pencil in my hand. Technology was just the stuff I used, not something I thought about working in.

## An unlikely starting point

My first real tech job was in support, answering calls in a high volume environment. I took it because I needed the work, not because it was a calling. The surprise was how much of it felt familiar. A call would come in with a problem I had never seen, and I had a short window to figure out what was going on and help the person through it. Underneath the stress, there was a puzzle, and I have always liked puzzles.

## The moment it clicked

What hooked me was not the technology itself at first. It was the feeling of taking something confusing and making it clear. A customer would start a call anxious or frustrated, and by the end they understood what happened and what to do next. I started staying curious after calls ended. I would look up why a fix worked instead of just noting that it worked. I read documentation nobody assigned me. I asked the engineers questions whenever I got the chance.

That curiosity kept compounding. The more I understood about how systems fit together, the more interesting the work got, and the better I got at it. Eventually I stopped thinking of support as a job I happened to land in and started treating it as a craft I wanted to keep improving.

## Still the same thread

People sometimes ask if I miss the art side of things. I do not think the two are as separate as they look. Drawing taught me patience and taught me to look closely before I act. Support uses both every day. My passion for technology did not arrive all at once. It grew out of paying attention to the work in front of me, and that is still how I try to approach it.
